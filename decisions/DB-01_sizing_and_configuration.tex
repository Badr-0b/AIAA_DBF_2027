\section{Summary}
\subsection{Purpose}
The configuration locks on 17~October and the design numbers freeze on 20~October. This brief brings the evidence for each open sizing and configuration decision into one place. For every decision it sets out the options, the supporting numbers and the risks, then gives a recommended option and the finding that would overturn it. Table~\ref{tab:summary} doubles as the team's decision record (Section~\ref{sec:decide}).

\subsection{Recommended baseline}
The analysis recommends a straight, flapped wing of 0.9~m² carrying a 4~kg payload unit, sized for a 14~m/s stall at Mission~2 weight, with Mission~1 waived and a closed-loop drum for sensor deployment and recovery. Across 120 draws of the uncertain inputs this design averages 6.33 of the 7.5 available mission points, and Mission~2 stays feasible in 98\,\% of draws.

\subsection{Basis}
The figures come from analysis Scripts 1 to 6 and the Table~06 deployment-and-recovery (D\&R) trade. The model carries the D\&R mechanism on every mission, as rule §3.1.2 requires. It is calibrated on 452 values verified against the 2021--26 top-3 AIAA design reports and on 513 rows of published fly-off results.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Figure of merit}
Designs are ranked by total mission score, $M_1 + M_2 + M_3 + GM$, which is capped at 7.5. Each mission is scored against the expected field, defined as 75\,\% of the per-mission specialist maxima anchored on the 2021--26 AIAA reports.

\subsection{Uncertainty}
Every design is evaluated on the same 120 random draws of the uncertain inputs (common random numbers), so any two designs are compared draw by draw. Three inputs are resampled from measured data:
\begin{itemize}
\item lap-time realism, from 24 flight pairs (median $\times$1.15);
\item pack specific energy, from 9 packs;
\item airframe mass, from the fit scatter ($\pm$31\,\% at one standard deviation).
\end{itemize}
Drag, efficiency, $C_{L,\max}$, air density and container mass are drawn from estimated ranges. ``Mean'' denotes the average over the draws, and P10 and P90 mark the boundaries of the worst and best tenth.

\subsection{Evidence grades}
Each input carries one of three grades:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{verified}: quoted from a primary source and machine-checked;
\item \emph{reseller}: taken from a retail listing only;
\item \emph{estimate}: a reasoned range, sampled in the Monte Carlo.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Model limits}
The model assigns no cost to take-off and landing risk, structural failure, build schedule or pilot skill. These factors enter through the risk register (Section~\ref{sec:risk}) and the team's judgment.

\section{Sizing}
\decision{D1}{Wing area and planform}
The robust design map (Fig.~\ref{fig:map}) scores every combination of wing area and payload unit over the 120 draws. The best point is a 0.9~m² wing with a 4~kg unit: a mean of 6.33, with Mission~2 feasible in 98\,\% of draws. Near-optimal designs form a plateau from 0.8 to 1.0~m². On the same draws, with each candidate at its best unit, the alternatives score as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item 1.0~m²: 6.29;
\item 0.8~m²: 6.23, with 1.0~m² ahead in 65\,\% of draws;
\item 0.6~m²: 5.78;
\item the 0.414~m² placeholder: 5.09;
\item the delta: 4.60.
\end{itemize}
The straight wing outscores both the placeholder and the delta in 99\,\% of draws.

The lower edge of the plateau carries a specific penalty. With the reel's 0.32~kg on board, a wing 10\,\% smaller loses the payload step, which costs 0.24 points in the tornado study. The wing should therefore be sized at 0.9~m² or slightly above.

The planform is straight and flapped, and the delta remains only as a comparison case. Taper ratio will be set from XFLR5 results (span efficiency and stall progression) together with manufacturing constraints. The team's planform matrix is retired because four of its five criteria were copied from the wing-position matrix.

\railnote{Recommendation}{A straight, flapped wing of 0.9~m², within a plateau of 0.9--1.0~m².}

\decision{D2}{Accepted stall speed}
The stall bound fixes the Mission~2 wing loading through $W/S = \rho V_s^2 C_{L,\max}/2$. At 14~m/s, with $\rho = 1.064$~kg/m³ and $C_{L,\max} = 1.6$, it gives 167~N/m². This is the largest single lever in the model (Fig.~\ref{fig:stall}, Table~\ref{tab:stall}): each 1~m/s above 14~m/s adds 0.13--0.15 to the mean score, and lowering the bound to 11~m/s costs 1.4.

The model applies no take-off distance limit and assigns no cost to landing risk, so this gain carries risk it leaves unpriced. The 2025 fly-off shows what is at stake.\footnote{Wing loadings derived from the quoted Mission~2 gross mass and wing area of each report (\texttt{analysis/data/dbf\_aircraft.toml}). UCLA: 18.73~lb on a 6~ft span with a 10.59~in mean chord (0.49~m²). Fly-off scores from \texttt{dbf\_results.csv}. The 17~m/s equivalent uses this brief's $\rho$ and $C_{L,\max}$.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The two heavy top-3 designs, UNSW and ERAU, flew at 248--257~N/m² (about 17~m/s) and both scored zero in Mission~2.
\item UCLA, at about 169~N/m², scored in every mission.
\end{itemize}

\railnote{Recommendation}{14~m/s. Raise the bound to 15~m/s (+0.13) only if the pilot signs off after seeing the landing speed.}

\decision{D3}{Payload unit and sensor}
Simulators must match the container plus the declared sensor to within $\pm$28.35~g (§3.1.3), so Mission~2 payload comes in whole units. The declared sensor mass also sets the ground-mission score. Table~\ref{tab:unit} shows the trade at 0.9~m².

The 4~kg unit gives both the best mean and the best P10. Heavier units raise the median and the ground-mission score, and an 8~kg unit reaches the GM ceiling of 1.50. They also make Mission~2 infeasible whenever the airframe comes in heavy. P(M2) falls from 98\,\% at 4~kg to 52\,\% at 8~kg, and P10 collapses to the floor set by Mission~1 and the ground mission alone. A failed Mission~2 also blocks Mission~3.

The declared sensor mass is fixed only at technical inspection in April (§4.1), which keeps the choice open. The payload bay should be sized now for two container-sized units, with the sensor mass chosen after the first weigh-in and flight tests.

\railnote{Recommendation}{A 4~kg unit carrying a 3.4~kg sensor, with simulators added as mass allows. Declare the sensor at technical inspection.}

\decision{D4}{Mission 1 waiver}
A successful Mission~2 also credits Mission~1, and flight attempts are scarce: teams that passed technical inspection received 2.4--2.9 official attempts each from 2022 to 2026. At the field's 65\,\% success rate per attempt, with three attempts, waiving Mission~1 raises the probability of completing Mission~3 from 40\,\% to 72\,\%. With four attempts the rise is from 70\,\% to 87\,\%. Every Script~6 number in this brief assumes the waiver.

The waiver gives up one fallback: if Mission~2 fails, no Mission~1 score remains to fall back on.

\railnote{Recommendation}{Waive Mission~1 and take its credit through Mission~2.}

\decision{D5}{Drop height}
The ground-mission score rises linearly with the declared drop height, up to 1.524~m (60~in). The container should be designed for 1.524~m. The team should then declare the greatest height that TC-02 passes cleanly with the real sensor, before the design report.

\railnote{Recommendation}{Design for 1.524~m and declare the height after TC-02.}

\section{Configuration}
\subsection{Field practice}
Field practice leaves the configuration open. Of the 19 verified top-3 aircraft since 2021, 13 flew a high wing, 13 a tricycle gear, 16 a conventional tail and 12 a single motor (Fig.~\ref{fig:practice}). No feature predicts fly-off success under Fisher's exact test:
\begin{itemize}
\item low wings completed Mission~3 in 5 of 6 cases, against 4 of 9 for high wings ($p = 0.29$);
\item single motors completed it in 7 of 10 cases, against 1 of 4 for twins ($p = 0.24$);
\item gear type shows no relationship at all ($p = 1.00$).
\end{itemize}
Each configuration choice therefore rests on physics, packaging and risk. The team's trade matrices were audited for arithmetic and evidence, then re-weighted with the Script~6 sensitivities. Table~\ref{tab:audit} gives the probability that each option ranks first over 5,000 perturbations.

\decision{D6}{Wing position}
A high wing wins 95\,\% of the audited trade. The audit corrected the teammates' low-wing payload score, because four low-wing top-3 designs (WashU 2022, UW 2023, and ERAU in 2023 and 2025) chose the low wing specifically for payload access. Three factors settle the final call: the sensor exit path, staging access through a top or bottom hatch (a Q\&A on the §3.3.2 hatch rule is pending) and the pilot's preference.

\railnote{Recommendation}{High wing, at medium confidence.}

\decision{D7}{Tail}
The as-made matrix chose a T-tail on line clearance. The tow line hangs 31--86° below horizontal for sensors of 0.5--5~kg, which makes clearance a function of where the line exits the fuselage, with the tail type playing little part. A T-tail's fin also carries the stabiliser loads, which makes it heavier and more prone to flutter. The clearance should be confirmed in CAD once the exit is placed.

\railnote{Recommendation}{Conventional tail, at medium confidence.}

\decision{D8}{Landing gear}
A retractable system pays for itself only below 120--240~g. That break-even is the fixed gear's drag area (9.9~cm² measured on Georgia Tech 2026; 19.4~cm² predicted for FH Joanneum 2022) multiplied by the exchange rate of 12.3~g per cm². Retracting would save at most 2.6--5.1~s of Mission~2 time, about 0.01 points, against the added risk of a gear-up landing. A fairing should reduce the fixed gear's drag further; its benefit has yet to be quantified from a verified source.

\railnote{Recommendation}{Fixed, faired tricycle gear, at medium confidence.}

\decision{D9}{Propulsion layout}
A nose-mounted tractor keeps the propeller disc clear of the tow line, whose slack moments during deployment and stowage are the SR-10 risk. The field also favours single motors (12 of 19), and twins completed Mission~3 in only 1 of 4 cases. The coming propulsion study (APC and UIUC data, a motor model, 12S against 8S) will confirm or overturn this choice.

\railnote{Recommendation}{Single tractor, provisional until the propulsion study.}

\decision{D10}{Sensor-bay closure}
Rule §3.1.1 requires an opening left uncovered in Mission~3 to stay uncovered in Missions~1 and~2. At the exchange rate of 10~cm² of drag area per 123~g, a door mechanism of an estimated 30--50~g pays for itself if the open slot's drag area exceeds 2.4--4.1~cm². A slot sized for a can-sized dart with fins probably exceeds that threshold, though no verified cavity-drag figure exists; WP-01 CFD should compute it. A door adds one failure mode, a door that stays shut and prevents deployment, which the latch test covers.

\railnote{Recommendation}{Fit a door unless CFD shows the open slot costs less than 2.4--4.1~cm².}

\decision{D11}{Body shape}
Through drag alone, body shape moves the score by hundredths of a point, since 10~cm² of drag area is worth 123~g. Mass and payload packaging decide it instead: the body must hold two container-sized units, the reel at the Mission~3 location and the sensor bay. TC-02 fixes the container size. A CAD packaging study then gives the smallest body, and any outer form (whale-like, MQ-4C-like or a plain tube) is scored on the same drag-and-mass scale (\texttt{config\_exchange.pdf}).

\railnote{Status}{Deferred until TC-02 sets the container size.}

\section{Deployment and recovery}
\decision{D12}{Screening}
Eight concepts were eliminated against the rules or against physics:
\begin{itemize}
\item a receiver on the drum, ruled out by the §3.2.1 external switch;
\item a pulley block, which needs 77--153~s to deploy against a 20~s window;
\item a boom, a wireless link and a two-line bridle, all ruled out by §3.1.1;
\item a spring reel;
\item a clock-spring lead, which would need 23 turns;
\item a sail-winch servo, which would need 2.3 times its rated torque.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Trade}
The four surviving candidates were sized with datasheet drives and compared pairwise on five criteria. Each criterion is weighted by the expected points it can swing:
\begin{itemize}
\item 100~g of mechanism mass costs 0.046 points;
\item 1~s of stow time costs 0.002 points;
\item each additional 1\,\% chance of losing the sensor costs 0.028 points.
\end{itemize}
Reliability therefore dominates the trade.

Option~A, the closed-loop drum, combines an encoder, a stop bead at the fairlead, a slip ring and a docking latch. The stop bead sets the 2.74~m deploy length and carries lap loads into the airframe. Option~A is the Condorcet winner. It wins 95--98\,\% of draws under three of the four decision rules and 71\,\% under the weighted Table~06 rule (Fig.~\ref{fig:dr}). The open-loop drum (B) is the runner-up: it is easier to build and wins 29\,\% under the weighted table, but it ties A only if its own failure rates fall below 46\,\% of the estimate.

\railnote{Recommendation}{Option A, the closed-loop drum, at medium-high confidence.}

\subsection{Open evidence}
The failure rates in the trade are engineering estimates. TC-05, a bench test card for the tow, reel and slip ring, must replace them with counted cycles. The model carries the verified Pololu 37D 70:1 drive (a 0.32~kg mechanism with a 12~s stow at 3.4~kg). A brushless servo, on reseller data only, would save about 0.12~kg.
